Global Carbon Budget 2025 · 2015–2024 averages

The carbon that stays

The Mauna Loa curve does not measure what we emit. It measures what is left over after the oceans and vegetation have taken their share. Every year they absorb about half of it, for free.

The units on this page. GtCO₂ = billions of tonnes of CO₂. ppm = molecules of CO₂ per million molecules of air. Sink = whatever takes CO₂ out of the air: the ocean and vegetation. CO₂e = all greenhouse gases converted into the amount of CO₂ that would warm the same; used where methane and nitrous oxide count, as in the section on wars. ppm

The last time the air was like this

The bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice are real air from eight hundred thousand years ago, and it can be measured. In all that time CO₂ swung between 170 and 300 parts per million, back and forth, with the ice ages. Never once above.

No human being has ever breathed this air

The oldest fossils of our species are about 315,000 years old. In all that time CO₂ never went above 300 parts per million: today we are at 427. This is not a figure of speech, it is the record right here. Everything we call human history happened in the right-hand part of the chart, where the line is flat.

The further back you go, the less is measured

The ice reaches eight hundred thousand years: beyond that, ancient air no longer exists and you move to indirect indicators, which carry much wider uncertainties. The IPCC says today's CO₂ is the highest in at least two million years; a 2023 synthesis of the whole Cenozoic places the last time at this level around fourteen million years ago. Three different rulers, three answers: the measured one comes first.

The Pliocene is not a forecast

It is the row that matters, because the CO₂ back then is the CO₂ of today: between 360 and 420 ppm, for a long stretch. The world that came with it was 2.5–4 °C warmer and had the sea between 5 and 25 metres higher. But those values are equilibrium states, reached over millennia. The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets take thousands of years to respond, and the Pliocene tells you where the planet ends up if CO₂ stays there, not what happens by 2100. Projections for this century are another matter and give tens of centimetres, not tens of metres.

Why the ice ages are not an objection

«CO₂ has changed on its own before» is true, and you can see it clearly in the chart. Those cycles start from changes in the Earth's orbit, which by themselves move little energy: it is the CO₂ released by warming oceans that amplifies them into an ice age. The difference with today is the cause and the speed: those transitions added 100 ppm in about ten thousand years, we have added 140 in two hundred.

Three rulers, three answers

Ice cores are direct measurements of the air and reach eight hundred thousand years. To go further you need indirect indicators, such as boron in the shells of foraminifera: the IPCC, in 2021, concludes from these that current CO₂ is the highest in at least two million years. The Cenozoic synthesis published in Science in 2023 revised the reconstruction of the last 66 million years and places the last period with CO₂ steadily at these levels around fourteen million years ago. The number that often circulates, «three million years», comes from earlier reconstructions.

ppm / year

The last ten years, up close

On the long curve a single year is indistinguishable. Here it is not: and you can see that the years when CO₂ rose the most, 2016, 2023 and 2024, are the same years when the planet was warmest.

Why two different numbers for the same year

«Grew by 2.23 ppm» is the NOAA rate: how much the underlying curve rises between 1 January and 31 December. The difference between one year's mean and the previous year's gives a slightly different number (in 2024: 3.53 against 3.33), because an annual mean contains half of the previous year. This page always uses the NOAA rate.

°C

So what? What those ppm actually do

So far we have been counting molecules. More CO₂ in the air means less heat escaping to space: this is the same chart as before, measured in degrees.

One year above 1.5 °C is not the threshold crossed

The limit is judged on underlying warming, that is on an average of several years: here it is the last ten, today 1.18 °C. The Paris Agreement does not prescribe how many years, and the IPCC uses twenty. A single warm year can be pushed up by El Niño, as in 2024; a cooler year takes nothing back, as in 2025. The budget in the data box is how much we can still emit for a 50% chance of staying below 1.5 °C: 170 GtCO₂ from the start of 2026, minus the months already gone.

Go deeper: the thermometers disagree, and CO₂ is not alone

A tenth of a degree of disagreement between thermometers

This page uses NOAAGlobalTemp. Other authoritative series — ERA5 from Copernicus, HadCRUT from the Met Office, Berkeley Earth — give values between 1.5 and 1.6 °C for 2024 (ERA5 1.60; the World Meteorological Organization synthesis 1.55). The same NOAA series, in its previous version, gave 1.46: the 2025 revision lowered recent values by a few hundredths. The difference comes from how each one covers the areas without stations, above all the Arctic, and from how it estimates the pre-industrial period, for which measurements are few. When you read «we have passed 1.5 °C», it is worth asking with which ruler.

And carbon dioxide is not alone

CO₂ 425.6 ppm NOAA global mean, +52% above pre-industrial; Mauna Loa, further north, reads slightly higher
Methane · CH₄ 1936 ppb ppb = parts per billion · +168% above pre-industrial (722)
Nitrous oxide · N₂O 338.9 ppb +26% above pre-industrial (270)

Methane is far rarer but, molecule for molecule, it traps much more heat than CO₂ in the short term; in exchange it breaks down within about ten years, while a fraction of CO₂ stays for centuries. That is why cutting methane works fast and cutting CO₂ works forever: both are needed, and they are not interchangeable.

GtCO₂ / year

Where what we emit ends up

The global carbon budget has two sides. On the left what we put in, on the right the three possible destinations. Only the third one shows up in the curve.

The point of this page

If the sinks stopped working tomorrow, with the same emissions the concentration would rise by 5.3 ppm a year instead of 2.6. Half the problem is already being swept under the carpet by oceans and forests. Per person that is about 5 tonnes a year, the weight of three cars.

The work the sinks have already done

One way to see what ocean and vegetation are worth: draw where the curve would be if they had absorbed nothing.

The free service, at a rising price

So far the sinks have absorbed more as we emitted more: more CO₂ in the air means more pressure pushing the gas into the ocean and more fertilisation of vegetation. But the Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates that warming has already weakened the land sink by 25% and the ocean sink by 7% compared with a world that had not warmed.

ppm / year

Why 2024 depends on where you look

In 2024 emissions grew by about 1%. At Mauna Loa CO₂ rose as much as the year before; in the global mean it made the biggest jump since the series began, in 1980. This is not a contradiction: they are two different instruments, and a single year is not enough to judge.

Why you should not trust a single year

2023 and 2024 were exceptional for a reason that has nothing to do with our emissions: El Niño dried the tropics and fed record fires, and vegetation absorbed less. The land sink held 7.4 GtCO₂ in 2023 and 7.1 in 2024, against a decade average of 8.7. The ocean, far more stable, carried on doing its job.

Go deeper: the 2024 that does not add up, and how it ended in 2025

For 2024 the budget does not add up: summing what ended in the air, in the sea and in vegetation gives more than what appears to have been emitted, with a gap of 6 GtCO₂, the most negative in the series. The Global Carbon Project publishes it as an item of its own. In 2025 growth fell to 2.23 ppm at Mauna Loa and 2.06 in the global mean, and the preliminary Global Carbon Budget estimate indicates that the land sink is back to its former strength. The anomaly was a swing, not a break: building a thesis on 2024 alone would be unwise in either direction.

Is it «just China»?

No for the level, largely yes for the growth. China accounts for a third of today's total. Of the increase over the last twenty-five years it accounts for about two thirds, because in the meantime Europe and the United States have come down.

Two warnings before quoting it

The first: «net» hides something. Europe and the United States also came down by moving production to China itself. Counted on consumption rather than production, a sizeable slice of Chinese emissions is goods destined for us, and the European fall gets much thinner.

The second: the picture flips if you look at the carbon already accumulated instead of the annual flow. There the Western contribution is still the largest, and will stay so for a good while.

MtCO₂e

How much the wars weigh

Two counter-intuitive answers. Armies in peacetime emit far more than the wars they fight. And inside a war the weapons weigh almost nothing: what counts is what they hit, and what then has to be rebuilt. Together they are worth about 5% of the world's greenhouse gases. These are the least solid estimates on this page, and they are floors, not measurements.

How much it comes to in a year

Five squares out of a hundred. That is the combined weight of the peacetime military apparatus, 4.8%, and the two wars for which a count exists, 0.2%, out of the 57.7 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases the world emitted in 2024. The first figure has a wide range, between 2.8 and 6.1%; the second is almost all Ukraine, and does not include the conflicts nobody has counted.

Why they are floors, not measurements

In 2024 there were 61 active armed conflicts, the highest number since the end of the Second World War. Two have a carbon count: Ukraine and Gaza. For Iran there is a preliminary estimate; for Syria, Lebanon, Yemen and Sudan, nothing. Armies for their part do not report: the first exemptions came with the Kyoto Protocol in 1997, since 2015 the Paris Agreement makes reporting voluntary, and the United States, China and Russia do not publish complete data. And even where a count exists, it counts greenhouse gases alone.

In Ukraine too the weapons are the small part

Fighting is little more than a third of the total. The rest is what the war drags behind it: rebuilding, failing to put out the fires, diverting aircraft, moving people. Reconstruction alone weighs almost as much as the fighting, and grows as the destruction piles up. At 185 dollars a tonne, the climate damage Ukraine intends to claim exceeds 57 billion.

Israel and Gaza: 93% is the aftermath

The same pattern, even sharper. Military operations are worth a little over 1.3 MtCO₂e up to January 2025; the total reaches 33.2 counting the barriers built before and the reconstruction to come: almost 450 thousand flats, over 3,000 kilometres of roads, schools, hospitals, water systems. If the number looks small next to Ukraine it is because the territory is tiny and the count of operations is narrow: it covers flights and vehicles, not every building that burned.

Iran: what was hit, and what the count is missing

Around thirty oil processing and storage plants in Iran, plus refineries and tankers outside the country: that is where the 1.88 million tonnes of the «oil burned» item come from. The count covers the first two weeks and has not yet been through a scientific journal: we have marked it as a preliminary estimate in the chart. Take it for the order of magnitude and for the proportions between items, not for the exact figure.

The Syrian paradox

During the war the emissions declared by Syria fell, because oil production collapsed by more than a quarter. Meanwhile the damage was piling up elsewhere: putting the destroyed houses back up alone is worth about 22 MtCO₂, and it will arrive in the years of reconstruction. In official accounts a war can look like a climate policy, which is another reason why the share alongside is a floor.

What no count includes

Even the best accounts measure one thing only, greenhouse gases. Left out are the mines that make fields unfarmable, the heavy metals in the soil — lead, zinc, chromium, arsenic around Sumy —, the PFAS and asbestos lifted from the rubble, the dioxins of the fires, the crude that ended up in the Persian Gulf. That is not climate, it is a different damage, and none of the percentages on this page include it. Vegetation fires are left out too: in the sixteen countries with ongoing or recent conflicts, in 2020 alone, 1,456 MtCO₂e went into the air. Not all of it caused by war, but attributed to nobody.

A precedent: the Kuwait wells

In 1991, retreating from Kuwait, the Iraqi army set fire to hundreds of oil wells. Those fires alone were worth more than 2% of all the fossil CO₂ emitted in the world that year: more than four years of war in Ukraine have produced. It is the limiting case that explains why hitting energy, rather than people, is what weighs most on the climate.

How those two percentages are built

The denominator is the world total of all greenhouse gases in 2024, 57.7 GtCO₂e, from the UNEP emissions gap report. The 4.8% is the 2,750 MtCO₂e of the armies: the authors of the estimate write 5.5%, computed on an older and lower world total, and the substance does not change. The 0.2% is the sum of what is attributed to each year of war: 78 MtCO₂e for Ukraine, four years divided by four, and 27 for the conflict between Israel and Gaza. The two weeks in Iran are worth another 5 MtCO₂e, a thousandth of the world total, and cannot be annualised.

Does the armies' 5.5% include the wars? No

It is the question that number drags behind it, and the answer changes what the sum means. The 4.8% is the apparatus that stands there anyway: fuel for vehicles, ships and aircraft, the bases, and above all the industry that builds and supplies them. Of that total only a fifth, 1% of world emissions, is fuel burned by the armed forces; the rest is the supply chain. The authors of the estimate write that it is conservative precisely because it leaves out the emissions of the fighting: what happens when the shooting starts is not in there.

The wars' 0.2% counts exactly that: fuel spent in battle, munitions, destroyed equipment to replace, burned buildings, reconstruction. The two figures measure different things, which is why they can be added without counting anything twice. A grey zone does exist — an army at war burns more than an army in barracks, and that extra could show up on both sides — but it is small next to the range of the first figure, which runs from 2.8 to 6.1%.

And military spending is rising

Every 100 billion dollars of extra military spending is worth about 32 MtCO₂e a year. The increase in NATO spending between 2019 and 2024 has already added around sixty, and the announced increases would bring another 132: as much as the fossil emissions of a country like Chile, every year. The figures have a wide margin, the sign does not.

1997–2012

Why Kyoto did not bend the curve

The protocol was not designed to stop the growth. And even if it had worked perfectly, the concentration would have risen anyway.

The physical reason

Atmospheric CO₂ is a stock, not a flow. It is a tub filling up: closing the tap halfway does not lower the level, it makes it rise more slowly. To stop the growth you need net zero emissions, that is not putting in more than the sinks take out: emitting less is not enough. Kyoto asked for minus 5.2% relative to 1990, for a subset of countries.

To be said anyway

Kyoto was not useless. It created the national inventories, the measurement and verification systems and the carbon markets, including the European emissions trading scheme (ETS). It is the infrastructure on which the Paris Agreement was then built. What it did not do is reverse or halt the growth of world emissions, and indeed it did not stop the rise of CO₂ in the atmosphere: precisely in the Kyoto years the concentration kept accelerating, from 1.5 to 2.0 ppm a year between the 1990s and the following decade. These are two separate accounts, and they should be kept separate. What would bend the curve: net zero emissions, not reduced emissions. That is the goal the Paris Agreement later set for mid-century.

in brief

Five things to take away

If one screen of this page is all that stays with you, let it be this one. The numbers below update together with those in the previous sections.

  1. CO₂ in the air is at 427.35 ppm, 53% above the pre-industrial level, and it is rising by about 2.6 ppm a year: three times faster than in the 1960s.
  2. Every year we emit about 41 billion tonnes; half disappears into ocean and vegetation. The curve measures only the other half. Without the sinks we would be at 574 ppm instead of 427.
  3. The planet warmed by +1.29 °C in the last year, +1.18 on average over the last decade. The Paris limit, 1.5 °C, is judged on an average of several years, not on a single one: it has not been passed yet, and about 140 GtCO₂ remain for a 50% chance of staying below it, less than four years of emissions at the current rate.
  4. The warm years are the years when CO₂ rises most. In 2023 and 2024 El Niño weakened vegetation and growth reached 3.3 ppm. A single year makes no trend, in either direction.
  5. Who emits depends on the question. Today China emits a third of the total, but an American emits one and a half times a Chinese person each, and over a hundred and seventy years the United States and Europe filled the tub more than anyone. Cutting slows the rise; to stop it you need net zero emissions.
What to watch a year from now

In January NOAA publishes the growth rate for the year just ended: the page reads it by itself. In November the Global Carbon Budget comes out, with the year's emissions and the estimate of the sinks: those numbers have to be updated by hand in the budget sections. At any time: the state of El Niño, because a new warm event would push CO₂ growth back up without emissions having changed.

Go deeper: conversions and two measures not to be confused

Conversions used on this page

1 ppm = 2.124 GtC = 7.78 GtCO₂
1 GtC = 3.664 GtCO₂

Two measures that should not be confused

The NOAA growth rate is the increase between 1 January and 31 December, computed on the thick line of the charts, the one without the seasonal breathing. The difference between annual means is another thing and gives different numbers: in 2024, 3.33 ppm the first, 3.53 the second. Also, Mauna Loa is not the planet: it is an observatory at 3,400 metres in the Pacific, while the NOAA global mean is computed from marine stations far from cities and industry, where the air is well mixed, combined so that each latitude band weighs as much as its surface. In 2024 the two measures give 3.33 and 3.76 ppm.

The GtC (billions of tonnes of carbon) that appear in scientific publications convert into GtCO₂ by multiplying by 3.664: this page always uses GtCO₂.

What is solid and what is not

Every figure carries a label saying what kind of number it is. observed are published measurements and inventories: the NOAA series, the country emissions of the Global Carbon Budget. observed and estimated is the global budget, where the fossil sources are inventories but the two sinks are reconstructed with models, which is the reason for the uncertainty whiskers under the blocks. estimate is a calculation or a model: the counterfactual without sinks, the accounting of wars. preliminary estimate is work that has not yet been through a scientific journal, and on this page it appears only once.

Fossil emissions are known to within about 5%. The ocean sink to within about 15%. The land sink and land use are the worst constrained items, with uncertainties that in single years exceed 50% of the value. The budget never closes exactly: the residual imbalance is published as an item of its own, and in 2024 it reached −6 GtCO₂ (−1.7 GtC), the most negative value in the series.

The cumulative figures for 1850–2024 concern fossil carbon only. Including historical land use the ranking changes significantly.

All values are rounded for readability. Before quoting one, check the exact figure at the primary source indicated above: the NOAA series and the Global Carbon Budget are revised with every edition.
